# RC 1 — THE VALUE OF FRICTION

### Passage

Modern organisations often treat friction as an obstacle to be eliminated. Processes are redesigned to reduce waiting, simplify choices and minimise the effort required to complete a task. Such efforts can indeed improve efficiency. Yet not every form of friction is wasteful. Some delays force people to reconsider decisions that would otherwise be made impulsively; some procedural requirements expose errors before they become costly.

The difficulty lies in distinguishing productive friction from unnecessary obstruction. A system that requires approval at every minor stage may simply slow work without improving its quality. But a system that inserts a deliberate pause before an irreversible decision can serve a valuable function precisely because it interrupts the momentum toward action.

Technology complicates this distinction. Automated systems can remove small inconveniences so effectively that users may become less aware of the decisions they are making. The resulting experience feels efficient, but efficiency here may conceal a transfer of responsibility from the user to the system's designers.

The appropriate goal, therefore, is not frictionlessness. It is intelligent friction: enough resistance to encourage reflection where reflection matters, but not so much that routine activity becomes unnecessarily burdensome. Designing such systems requires understanding not only how long a process takes but also what the pauses within it accomplish.

# RC 2 — THE POLITICS OF CATEGORIES

### Passage

Categories help people make sense of a complicated world. By grouping objects, behaviours or people according to shared characteristics, categories reduce the cognitive effort required to process information. A doctor, for instance, can organise an enormous amount of clinical information more efficiently by classifying symptoms into recognised patterns.

Yet categories do more than simplify. They also influence what becomes noticeable. Once a classification is established, observations that fit its boundaries may receive more attention than observations that fall between categories. This does not mean that categories are inherently misleading. Without them, systematic knowledge would often be impossible. The problem arises when a category designed for one purpose is treated as though it captures reality completely.

The risk is particularly significant when categories become institutionalised. A classification used initially for administrative convenience may later determine how resources are allocated, how research questions are framed, or how people understand themselves. The category then ceases to be merely descriptive and begins to shape the environment it was intended to describe.

A sensible approach is therefore neither to reject categories nor to treat them as natural facts. Categories should be understood as tools: useful because they simplify, but limited because every simplification highlights some features and suppresses others. Their usefulness depends partly on recognising the purpose for which they were created and the consequences of extending them beyond that purpose.

\[

New average:

\[
12\times18.5=222.

\[
222-216=6.

\]

### ⚠️ Validation result

**Q1 is internally inconsistent as written.**

For a group of 12 students, replacing one student by someone 4 years older can increase the total age by exactly 4, changing the average from 18 to:

\[
18+\frac4{12}=18\frac13,
\]

not 18.5.

**Do not score Q1.**

This is exactly the sort of mismatch that should be caught before scoring rather than forcing a solution.

**Common trap:** Solving algebraically without first checking whether the stated averages are compatible.
