# RC 1 — WHEN SIMPLIFICATION HELPS

### Passage

A representation cannot reproduce everything about the thing it represents. A map omits many geographical details; a scientific model leaves out countless physical variables; a statistical measure compresses a complex population into a smaller set of numbers. It is tempting, therefore, to regard simplification as a loss of accuracy and consequently of usefulness.

That conclusion, however, confuses completeness with fitness for purpose. A subway map may distort physical distance while preserving the sequence and connectivity of stations. For a passenger deciding how to travel, these relationships can matter more than geographical scale. Similarly, a scientific model may deliberately ignore details that are irrelevant to the prediction it is intended to make.

Simplification is not therefore automatically a virtue either. If a representation omits or distorts a relationship essential to its purpose, the simplification undermines its usefulness. The appropriate question is not how much information a representation contains, but whether it preserves what its intended user needs to know.

# RC 2 — THE METRIC IS NOT THE GOAL

### Passage

Organisations frequently translate broad objectives into measurable indicators. This makes performance easier to monitor, compare and reward. Yet measurement introduces a risk: once an indicator becomes attached to incentives, people may begin optimising the indicator rather than the broader objective it was designed to represent.

Consider a company that wants to improve customer service and rewards employees according to the number of calls they handle. An employee who increases call volume may be responding perfectly rationally to the incentive system. The problem lies not necessarily with the employee but with the assumption that call volume is a sufficiently accurate proxy for service quality.

Metrics therefore occupy an intermediate position between an objective and the evidence used to judge progress toward it. Their usefulness depends on the strength of the connection between the metric and the objective. When that connection weakens, an organisation can register numerical improvement without achieving corresponding substantive improvement.

# 🔥 DAY 86 — 99+ PERCENTILE SHORTCUT BANK

### 1. Percentage multiplier

For successive changes:

\[
\boxed{\text{Final factor}=\prod(1\pm r)}
\]

Never add the percentages.

---

### 2. Average questions

If the number of observations remains unchanged:

\[
\boxed{\Delta\text{average}=\frac{\Delta\text{total}}{n}}
\]

Q3 becomes almost instant:

\[
\frac{18-30}{8}=-1.5.
\]

So:

\[
24-1.5=22.5.
\]

---

### 3. Absolute-value equality

Whenever you see:

\[
|A|=|B|,
\]

immediately think:

\[
\boxed{A=B\text{ or }A=-B}.
\]

Don't square unless necessary.

---

### 4. Circle chord

Always halve the chord:

\[
\boxed{\text{radius}^2
=
\left(\frac{\text{chord}}2\right)^2
+
(\text{distance from centre})^2}
\]

Look for Pythagorean triples.

---

### 5. Divisor counting

For:

\[
N=p^aq^br^c,
\]

ordinary divisor count:

\[
(a+1)(b+1)(c+1).
\]

If the divisor must contain \(p^kq^l\), simply change the exponent ranges:

\[
p:k\ldots a,\qquad q:l\ldots b.
\]

---

### 6. Permutation with zero

The moment 0 appears in a number-formation question:

> **Ask whether 0 can occupy the first position.**

If not, separate the first position.

This is one of the highest-frequency traps in CAT permutation questions.

---

### 7. Integer optimisation

For fixed sum:

\[
xyz
\]

is largest near equality.

First calculate:

\[
\boxed{\left(\frac{x+y+z}{3}\right)^3}
\]

as an upper bound.

Only then apply divisibility restrictions.

For Q12:

\[
xyz\le729,
\]

so the answer cannot possibly be 750.

That one observation eliminates D **without solving anything**.
