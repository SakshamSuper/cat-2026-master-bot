### Passage

Institutions frequently respond to uncertainty by collecting more information. The logic appears straightforward: uncertainty is reduced when more facts become available. Yet information does not automatically reduce uncertainty. Facts must be relevant to the decision, reliable enough to support inference, and organised in a way that permits meaningful interpretation.

Indeed, an excess of information can sometimes make judgment harder. When decision-makers face numerous signals without a framework for distinguishing important information from incidental details, the additional material can increase cognitive burden rather than improve understanding.

This does not justify restricting information indiscriminately. The problem is not abundance itself, but the failure to distinguish between information that expands understanding and information that merely expands volume. Effective decision-making therefore depends as much on the organisation and interpretation of information as on its collection.

### Passage

Technological innovation is often remembered through highly visible products: the first commercially successful electric vehicle, the first smartphone, or a dramatic new computing platform. Such narratives are attractive because they give technological change a clear beginning and a recognisable protagonist. Yet they can obscure the less visible changes that make such products possible.

Infrastructure, supply chains, manufacturing processes and organisational capabilities often develop gradually. Innovation, therefore, should not be understood only as the appearance of a new product. It can also consist of the accumulation of enabling changes whose importance becomes visible only retrospectively.

\]

\boxed{x=10\%}.

## Q4 — **B, 5**

Let the roots be \(r\) and \(r+4\).

Their product:

\[
r(r+4)=k.
\]

Their sum:

\[
2r+4=k+1.
\]

Therefore:

\[
k=2r+3.
\]

Substitute:

\[
r^2+4r=2r+3
\]

\[
r^2+2r-3=0
\]

\[
(r-1)(r+3)=0.
\]

Positive root:

\[
r=1.
\]

Thus:

\[
k=2(1)+3=5.
\]

\[
\boxed B
\]

**Source:** CAT-level inspired  
**Topic:** Quadratic roots  
**Difficulty:** 4/5  
**Recommended time:** 1.5 min  

**Shortcut:** Use:

\[
\alpha+\beta=k+1,\quad\alpha\beta=k
\]

and set \(\beta=\alpha+4\).

**Common trap:** Assuming the roots themselves must be \(k\) and 1 without verifying.

**Key concept:**

\[
\alpha+\beta=-\frac ba,\qquad
\alpha\beta=\frac ca.
\]

**Alternate:** Test integer pairs differing by 4 and compare sum/product.
