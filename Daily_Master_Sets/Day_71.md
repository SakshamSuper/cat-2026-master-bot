# RC 1 — MODELS AND THEIR BOUNDARIES

### Passage

A model is useful partly because it refuses to reproduce reality in its entirety. By selecting a limited number of relationships, a model makes patterns easier to identify and manipulate. This selectivity is therefore not an accidental defect but part of what makes modelling possible in the first place.

The difficulty arises when the success of a model in one context encourages confidence in contexts for which it was never designed. A model that isolates one relationship may illuminate that relationship precisely because it temporarily ignores others. Once the model is treated as a complete description, however, those exclusions cease to appear as deliberate simplifications and begin to look like features of reality itself.

This does not imply that models should become increasingly elaborate. Adding variables can introduce new assumptions and may obscure the relationship that the original model was intended to clarify. The relevant criterion is not how much a model contains, but whether what it excludes is consequential for the question being asked.

Good modelling therefore requires two kinds of discipline: abstraction sufficient to make a problem tractable, and awareness sufficient to prevent the abstraction from being mistaken for the whole phenomenon.

# RC 2 — CHOICE ARCHITECTURE

### Passage

People often think of choice as an entirely individual act: a person has preferences, encounters alternatives and selects one of them. Yet choices do not occur in a vacuum. The order in which alternatives are presented, the effort required to discover them and the prominence given to particular options can all affect what people ultimately choose.

Recognising this influence does not require the conclusion that individuals lack agency. A person can reject a default option, search for alternatives or deliberately ignore a recommendation. But agency has a cost structure. If one option is immediately visible while another requires several additional steps to locate, choosing the latter involves a different expenditure of effort even when both remain technically available.

This is particularly important in digital environments, where interfaces can continuously rearrange the practical landscape of choice. A recommendation system may not compel a user to select an item, but it can make some possibilities easier to encounter than others. Over time, repeated exposure can influence familiarity, and familiarity can in turn influence preference.

The important question is therefore not whether environments determine individual choices. They generally do not. Rather, it is how the design of an environment changes the relative ease with which different choices can be noticed, considered and pursued.
