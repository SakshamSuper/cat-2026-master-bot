### Passage

Maps are often treated as neutral representations of geographical reality. Yet every map necessarily selects what to include and what to omit. A map designed for navigation may emphasise roads and landmarks, while one designed for geological analysis may make entirely different features prominent. Neither map is necessarily more truthful in an absolute sense; each is constructed for a particular purpose.

The apparent objectivity of a map can therefore conceal a series of choices. Decisions about scale, symbols, boundaries and labels affect what users notice and how they interpret spatial relationships. These choices do not make maps useless or deceptive. Rather, they remind us that a representation can be accurate for a purpose without being exhaustive.

The important question, therefore, is not whether a map reproduces reality completely, but whether the features it highlights are appropriate to the question for which the map is being used.

### Passage

Technological standards often appear to be technical matters, determined by efficiency or performance. In practice, however, standards can have effects far beyond engineering. Once a particular standard becomes widely adopted, producers, consumers and complementary technologies begin adapting to it.
