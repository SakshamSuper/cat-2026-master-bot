# RC 1 — THE VALUE OF CONSTRAINTS

### Passage

A common assumption about creativity is that it flourishes when restrictions are removed. The reasoning appears intuitive: if individuals have unlimited choices, they should have greater freedom to produce novel ideas. Yet unlimited choice can create its own difficulty. When every possibility is available, deciding where to begin may become harder than generating alternatives.

Constraints can therefore play a productive role in creative work. A fixed format, limited budget or specific audience can narrow the space of possible solutions and force attention toward the problem's essential features. This does not mean that stronger restrictions always produce better ideas. Excessive constraints may prevent experimentation altogether. Their value depends on whether they eliminate irrelevant possibilities while preserving enough room for meaningful variation.

The productive role of constraints is consequently less paradoxical than it first appears. Creativity does not necessarily require an absence of boundaries; it may require boundaries that focus attention without eliminating agency.

# RC 2 — INSTITUTIONS AND TRUST

### Passage

Trust in institutions is sometimes discussed as though it were primarily a matter of public opinion: if citizens believe that an institution is trustworthy, the institution is considered successful. But this view risks confusing perception with the mechanisms that generate justified trust. An institution can temporarily enjoy high approval while operating in ways that are opaque or unaccountable.

Durable institutional trust requires more than favourable perceptions. It depends on procedures that allow decisions to be scrutinised, errors to be acknowledged and power to be constrained. These mechanisms may occasionally expose institutional failures, thereby reducing confidence in the short term. Yet such transparency can strengthen trust over a longer period because it demonstrates that the institution is capable of correction.

The apparent paradox is that institutions may sometimes preserve trust not by protecting their image from evidence of failure, but by making their response to failure visible. Trust, in this sense, is not simply confidence that an institution will never fail; it is confidence that the institution possesses mechanisms for recognising and correcting failure.
