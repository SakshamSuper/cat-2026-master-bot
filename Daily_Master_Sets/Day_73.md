# RC 1 — THE LIMITS OF MEASUREMENT

### Passage

Measurement appears to offer a powerful route to objectivity. Once a phenomenon is assigned a numerical value, comparisons become easier and disagreements can appear more manageable. Yet the numerical form of a measurement can conceal decisions that preceded it: what exactly is being measured, which features count, and how those features are translated into a scale.

This does not make measurement arbitrary. A well-designed measure can reveal patterns that would otherwise remain difficult to detect. The problem arises when the precision of the number is mistaken for precision in the underlying concept. A measure can be highly consistent while still capturing only one dimension of a much richer phenomenon.

Consequently, the value of measurement depends not simply on numerical accuracy but on the relationship between the measure and the question it is intended to answer. Numbers are most useful when their limitations are understood rather than hidden behind an appearance of exactness.

# RC 2 — TECHNOLOGY AND ATTENTION

### Passage

Digital technologies are often criticised for fragmenting attention, but the criticism can become too simple if it assumes that technology acts independently of human intentions. People do not merely receive digital environments passively; they choose applications, establish routines and develop strategies for managing interruptions. At the same time, however, the architecture of digital systems can make some patterns of attention easier to sustain than others.

Notifications provide a useful example. A notification does not compel a person to respond, yet it introduces a competing demand at a moment selected by the system. Repeated interruptions may gradually alter habits even when each individual interruption is easily ignored. The relevant issue is therefore not whether technology controls attention completely, but how technological environments redistribute the costs of maintaining attention.

This distinction matters because it avoids two simplistic positions. One treats users as powerless victims of technology; the other treats attention as entirely independent of technological design. In reality, attention is shaped through an interaction between individual practices and environmental structures.
