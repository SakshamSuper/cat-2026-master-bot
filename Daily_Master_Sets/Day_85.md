# RC 1 — THE VALUE OF SIMPLIFICATION

### Passage

A representation necessarily leaves something out. A map cannot preserve every geographical feature, a scientific model cannot reproduce every detail of the phenomenon being studied, and a statistical measure cannot capture every aspect of the population it describes. Yet omission is not automatically a defect. The question is what the representation is designed to accomplish.

A subway map, for example, may greatly distort geographical distances while accurately preserving the sequence and connectivity of stations. For a passenger deciding how to travel, these preserved relationships may matter far more than physical distance. Similarly, a scientific model may deliberately ignore details that have little relevance to the prediction it is designed to make.

This does not mean that accuracy becomes irrelevant. A representation becomes defective when its simplifications distort a relationship that is essential to its intended use. The point is therefore not that less detail is always better, but that detail should be evaluated according to purpose. A representation can be useful precisely because it refuses to reproduce everything.

# RC 2 — METRICS AND OBJECTIVES

### Passage

Organisations frequently convert broad objectives into measurable indicators. Doing so makes performance easier to compare, monitor and reward. But measurement introduces a subtle danger: the indicator can gradually be mistaken for the objective it was intended to represent.

Suppose a company wants to improve customer service and rewards employees according to the number of calls they handle. Employees who respond by increasing call volume may not be behaving irrationally. They are responding to the incentive system the organisation has created. The difficulty is that call volume may be an imperfect proxy for service quality: more calls handled does not necessarily mean customers received better service.

Metrics are therefore best understood as proxies rather than complete definitions of success. Their usefulness depends on whether changes in the measured quantity continue to correspond reasonably with changes in the underlying objective. When that relationship weakens, an organisation can observe impressive numerical performance while making little meaningful progress.

# 🔥 DAY 85 — 99+ PERCENTILE SHORTCUT BANK

### 1. Mixtures — use allegation before equations

For:

\[
30\%,40\%,50\%
\]

the deviations from the target are equal:

\[
40-30=50-40.
\]

Therefore quantities must be equal.

This turns a 3-line equation into a **5-second observation**.

---

### 2. Train questions — subtract the times

If the same train:

- crosses a pole in \(t_1\),
- crosses a platform in \(t_2\),

then:

\[
\boxed{\text{platform length}
=\text{speed}\times(t_2-t_1)}
\]

because the train length cancels.

---

### 3. Vieta is a major CAT weapon

If roots are \(\alpha,\beta\), immediately record:

\[
\boxed{\alpha+\beta=-\frac ba}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{\alpha\beta=\frac ca}.
\]

Then use identities rather than solving.

---

### 4. Modulus — think geometrically

For:

\[
|x-a|+|x-b|,
\]

the minimum possible value is:

\[
|a-b|.
\]

For Q5:

\[
|2-(-4)|=6.
\]

Since the required value is 10, we need to move:

\[
10-6=4
\]

units beyond either endpoint:

\[
x=-6,\;4.
\]

This is considerably faster than piecewise algebra.

---

### 5. Divisor restrictions

If:

\[
N=2^a3^b5^c
\]

and the divisor must contain:

\[
2^33^2,
\]

don't calculate all divisors first.

Directly count:

\[
2:\;3\ldots a
\]

\[
3:\;2\ldots b
\]

\[
5:\;0\ldots c.
\]

---

### 6. Repeated-letter permutations

For BALLOON:

\[
\frac{7!}{2!2!}
\]

because L and O are repeated.

For “L not together”:

\[
\boxed{\text{Total}-\text{LL together}}.
\]

The most common CAT trap is forgetting the repeated O **inside the LL-block calculation**.

---

### 7. Probability — translate words into cases

“At least two red” means:

\[
\boxed{\text{exactly 2}+\text{exactly 3}}
\]

not just exactly 2.

---

### 8. 99+ optimisation reflex

For positive \(x,y,z\) with fixed sum:

\[
xyz\le
\left(\frac{x+y+z}{3}\right)^3.
\]

Always calculate this ceiling **before touching the options**.

For Q12:

\[
xyz\le10^3=1000.
\]

Then the divisibility condition tells you to search near:

\[
(10,10,10),
\]

rather than checking arbitrary triples.
