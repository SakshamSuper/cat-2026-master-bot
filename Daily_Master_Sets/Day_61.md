# RC 1 — THE VALUE OF FORGETTING

### Passage

Modern systems often treat memory as an asset whose value increases with preservation. Digital storage makes it possible to retain photographs, messages, documents and records indefinitely, creating the impression that forgetting is merely a defect that technology can eliminate. Yet human memory has never functioned simply as a storage device. Forgetting can help determine which experiences remain salient and which cease to compete for attention.

The ability to discard information is especially important when accumulated detail interferes with generalisation. Someone who remembers every individual example equally may struggle to recognise the broader pattern those examples collectively reveal. Forgetting some particulars can therefore support abstraction rather than undermine it.

This does not mean that forgetting is always beneficial. Losing information that remains relevant can impose substantial costs, and institutions often depend on accurate records precisely because individual memory is unreliable. The point is instead that retention and forgetting perform different functions. A system designed solely to maximise retention may preserve information while making it harder to determine what deserves attention.

The challenge, then, is not simply to remember more. It is to develop mechanisms—human or technological—that help distinguish information worth preserving from information whose continued prominence creates noise.

# RC 2 — WHEN EFFICIENCY BECOMES THE GOAL

### Passage

Efficiency is usually understood as the achievement of a desired result with fewer resources. The definition appears straightforward until the desired result itself is influenced by what can be measured. Once organisations begin rewarding what is easily quantifiable, employees may gradually redirect effort toward measurable outputs even when those outputs are only imperfect proxies for the underlying objective.

This phenomenon does not require anyone to behave irrationally. If promotion depends on a particular metric, responding to that metric can be entirely rational. The difficulty arises when the metric becomes the target rather than evidence about the target. A hospital, for example, might seek to reduce waiting times because shorter waits usually indicate better service. But if waiting time becomes the dominant performance measure, other dimensions of care may receive less attention.

The problem, therefore, is not measurement itself. Without measurement, organisations may find it difficult to coordinate activity or evaluate performance. The problem is confusing the instrument with the objective. A useful metric should illuminate a goal without becoming a substitute for the goal.

Good organisational design consequently requires some tolerance for outcomes that are difficult to measure. What cannot be reduced to a convenient number should not automatically be treated as unimportant.
