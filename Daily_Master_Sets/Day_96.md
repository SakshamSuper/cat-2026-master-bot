# RC 1 — THE VALUE OF REDUNDANCY

### Passage

Redundancy is often treated as waste. In systems designed for maximum efficiency, repeated components, duplicate procedures and spare capacity appear undesirable because they consume resources without producing an immediate additional output. Yet systems that operate under uncertainty cannot be evaluated solely by how efficiently they function under normal conditions.

A backup component may remain unused for years and therefore appear economically irrational. Its value becomes visible only when the primary component fails. Similarly, maintaining a reserve of time or capacity may look inefficient during ordinary periods but can prevent a minor disruption from becoming a systemic breakdown.

The challenge, therefore, is not to maximise redundancy indiscriminately. Excessive duplication can certainly become costly and counterproductive. Rather, the relevant question is whether the cost of redundancy is justified by the consequences of failure and the uncertainty surrounding that failure. What looks like inefficiency from the perspective of ordinary operation may constitute resilience from the perspective of the whole system.

# RC 2 — THE PROBLEM WITH SIMPLE METRICS

### Passage

Metrics are attractive because they convert complicated phenomena into quantities that can be compared. A school can be ranked by examination scores, a hospital by waiting times, and a business by quarterly revenue. Such measures can be useful precisely because they simplify decisions that would otherwise require extensive investigation.

But a metric becomes problematic when the measured quantity is mistaken for the phenomenon itself. Improving an examination score, for instance, may involve teaching students strategies for that examination rather than increasing their broader understanding. A hospital that reduces waiting time may do so by discouraging difficult cases rather than improving treatment.

This does not make metrics useless. The problem is instead one of interpretation. A metric should be treated as evidence about a larger phenomenon rather than as a complete representation of it. The more consequential the decision attached to a metric, the greater the need to examine what the metric captures—and what it leaves out.
