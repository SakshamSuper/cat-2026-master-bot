# RC 1 — THE LIMITS OF METRICS

### Passage

The modern fascination with measurement rests on a seemingly reasonable premise: what can be quantified can be compared, and what can be compared can be improved. Yet measurement does not merely describe an activity. Once a metric becomes a target, it can begin to alter the activity itself. A school judged primarily by examination scores may devote more time to test preparation; a company evaluated by quarterly sales may prioritise transactions whose benefits are immediately visible.

This does not make measurement inherently misleading. Without measurement, many decisions would depend entirely on intuition, memory or anecdote. The problem arises when a proxy is mistaken for the thing it represents. A metric is necessarily a selective representation of reality. It captures some dimensions of an outcome while leaving others outside its field of vision.

The resulting difficulty is not solved simply by adding more metrics. Several imperfect measures can still encourage behaviour that optimises what is easiest to count. What matters is understanding the relationship between the measure and the underlying objective, including the possibility that people will adapt their behaviour once they know what is being measured.

Good measurement, therefore, requires more than numerical precision. It requires an awareness of what the metric excludes, how it might influence behaviour, and whether improving the measured quantity actually advances the broader objective.

# RC 2 — CULTURAL CONTINUITY

### Passage

Arguments about cultural preservation often assume that a culture is valuable precisely because it remains unchanged. On this view, alteration is evidence of dilution: once a language acquires new expressions, a ritual changes its form, or a craft adopts unfamiliar materials, something authentic is presumed to have been lost. Such reasoning, however, treats culture as an object rather than as a practice.

Practices survive partly because they are capable of being reinterpreted. A language remains useful when speakers adapt it to new technologies and social situations. A ritual may retain its significance even when some of its outward forms change. A craft can remain recognisably itself while incorporating materials or techniques that did not exist in an earlier period.

This does not mean that every change is harmless. A practice can be altered so extensively that the social meanings that sustained it disappear. The challenge, therefore, is not to identify an imaginary frozen version of a culture and preserve it unchanged, but to distinguish changes that allow a practice to remain socially meaningful from changes that sever it from the communities that give it significance.

Cultural continuity is thus better understood as continuity of meaning and practice rather than mere continuity of form. Preservation may sometimes require resisting change, but it may also require allowing change to occur.
