# RC 1 — THE POWER OF STANDARDS

### Passage

Standards are frequently imagined as neutral technical devices. A standard length, file format or measurement protocol appears to do little more than establish a common rule that allows different people or systems to coordinate. In practice, however, choosing a standard often involves deciding which features deserve priority.

Consider a digital file format. A format that preserves maximum detail may be ideal for archiving but inefficient for rapid transmission. Another format may sacrifice some information in exchange for speed and compatibility. Neither choice is simply “correct” independently of purpose. The technical standard embodies a decision about which trade-off matters.

This does not mean that standards are merely expressions of political preference. Once adopted, they can generate substantial practical benefits: interoperability, lower transaction costs and predictable behaviour. Indeed, precisely because standards coordinate large numbers of actors, changing them can become expensive even when a technically superior alternative appears.

The significance of standards, therefore, lies partly in their ability to turn a particular choice into an infrastructure that subsequently appears natural. What was once a decision becomes a background condition of action. Recognising this does not require rejecting standards; it requires understanding that their apparent neutrality may conceal choices about priorities and trade-offs.

# RC 2 — URBAN TREES

### Passage

Urban trees are frequently defended in terms of their environmental services: reducing heat, absorbing pollutants and moderating stormwater. Such benefits are real, but treating trees primarily as pieces of environmental infrastructure can obscure another dimension of urban vegetation. Trees also shape how residents experience public space. Their shade, seasonal changes and physical presence can influence whether a street feels inviting, comfortable or even socially meaningful.

This experiential dimension complicates seemingly straightforward decisions about urban planting. A city might maximise cooling by planting one fast-growing species uniformly across a neighbourhood. Yet such uniformity can create ecological vulnerabilities and may also produce landscapes that residents find less distinctive or less connected to local history.

Nor does this imply that aesthetic or cultural considerations should override environmental performance. Rather, urban planning involves several objectives that may reinforce one another in some contexts and conflict in others. The mistake is to assume that a single metric can adequately capture the value of urban trees.

A richer approach would therefore treat urban vegetation as simultaneously ecological, social and cultural infrastructure. Such an approach does not make decisions easier; it makes explicit why they are difficult. But recognising the plurality of values involved can lead to choices that are more responsive to the actual ways in which cities are inhabited.

## Q1 — **₹28,000**

Let incomes be:

\[
5x,\ 7y
\]

and expenditures:

\[
3x,\ 4y.
\]

Therefore savings are:

\[
2x,\ 3y.
\]

Given:

\[
2x:3y=2:3.
\]

Hence:

\[
x=y.
\]

A's income:

\[
5x=20,000
\]

so:

\[
x=4,000.
\]

B's income:

\[
7x=7(4,000)=\boxed{28,000}.
\]

**Source:** CAT-level inspired  
**Topic:** Ratio / Income-Expenditure  
**Difficulty:** 4/5  
**Recommended time:** 2 min  

**Shortcut:** Match the savings ratio first. Here it immediately gives \(x=y\).

**Common trap:** Treating the three ratios independently without expressing savings as income minus expenditure.

**Key concept:** Always write:

\[
S=I-E.
\]

**Alternate method:** Assume income units \(5k,7k\), derive expenditure scale from the savings ratio.
