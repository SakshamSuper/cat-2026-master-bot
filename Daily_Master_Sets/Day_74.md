# RC 1 — THE PROBLEM WITH PRECISION

### Passage

Precision has considerable rhetorical power. A numerical statement can appear more authoritative than a qualitative one because its exactness suggests that the underlying matter has been settled. Yet precision in measurement and certainty in interpretation are not the same thing. A measurement can be extremely precise while the meaning assigned to that measurement remains contestable.

This distinction is particularly important when complex phenomena are reduced to a single indicator. The indicator may be useful precisely because it compresses information into a manageable form. But compression inevitably involves selection: some characteristics are retained while others disappear. The resulting number can therefore be valuable without being exhaustive.

The appropriate response is not to distrust precise measurements merely because they simplify reality. Rather, one must ask what the measure captures, what it excludes, and whether those choices are appropriate to the purpose for which the measure is being used. Precision is a property of a measurement; adequacy is a property of its relationship to a question.

# RC 2 — CHOICE AND ENVIRONMENT

### Passage

The idea of individual choice is often presented as though choices occur in a neutral space. Yet the environment in which a choice is made can affect which options receive attention, how much effort they require, and how their consequences are perceived. Recognising this influence does not require denying individual responsibility. It requires acknowledging that agency operates within conditions rather than outside them.

This becomes particularly visible in digital environments. A platform may present one action prominently, place another behind several menus, and repeatedly remind the user of a third. None of these arrangements necessarily prevents choice. Nevertheless, they alter the practical costs associated with different choices. A person may remain free to select any option while becoming more likely to select the option that is easiest to access.

The important distinction, then, is between freedom in the formal sense and equality of practical opportunity. Formal freedom asks whether alternatives remain available. Practical opportunity asks whether those alternatives are similarly accessible. The two questions overlap, but they are not identical.

## Q7 — \(\boxed C}\)

For a chord of length 16:

\[

\]

\[

\[

\[

\[

=\sqrt{289-225}

\[

\[
d_1^2-d_2^2=15^2-8^2=161.

\[
(d_1-d_2)(d_1+d_2)=161.

\[
d_1+d_2=17,

\[
d_1-d_2=\frac{161}{17}=\frac{161}{17}.

\[
161/17=9.4705\ldots
\]

which gives non-integral distances. More importantly, solve directly:

\[
d_1=\frac{17+161/17}{2},

which gives an impossible radius relative to the geometry.

Their sum cannot equal 17 for a real radius satisfying both chord constraints.

remember:
