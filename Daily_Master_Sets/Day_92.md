# RC 1 — EXPERTISE AND PATTERN RECOGNITION

### Passage

Expertise is often described as the accumulation of knowledge, but this description misses an important feature of expert performance. Experts do not merely possess more information; they organise it in ways that allow them to distinguish what is relevant from what is not. A novice may inspect many features of a problem without knowing which ones deserve priority, whereas an expert may focus quickly on a small number of decisive features.

This efficiency can look like intuition, but it need not be opposed to reasoning. Much of the reasoning has effectively been compressed into patterns acquired through experience. The expert's speed is therefore not necessarily evidence of shallow thought; it can instead be evidence of deeply internalised distinctions.

Yet the same mechanism can produce errors. A familiar pattern may encourage an expert to interpret a genuinely novel situation as though it were familiar. Expertise is consequently most reliable when rapid recognition is accompanied by a willingness to question whether the recognised pattern actually fits the circumstances.

# RC 2 — TECHNOLOGY AND INSTITUTIONAL CONTEXT

### Passage

Technological innovation is frequently described as a force that transforms institutions simply by making new forms of action possible. But possibility and adoption are not the same thing. Technologies enter environments in which rules, incentives and established habits already influence behaviour. Consequently, a technology may make decentralised decision-making possible without causing an organisation to decentralise.

This helps explain why the same technological system can have different consequences in different organisations. Where existing incentives reward central control, the technology may reinforce that arrangement; where incentives reward delegation, it may facilitate decentralisation. The technology creates an opportunity, but institutional conditions influence whether that opportunity becomes routine practice.

Understanding technological change therefore requires more than studying technical capabilities. It requires examining the institutional environment that filters, encourages or constrains the uses to which those capabilities are put.
