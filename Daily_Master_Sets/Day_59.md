# RC 1 — THE PROBLEM WITH PERFECT PREDICTION

### Passage

The appeal of prediction lies partly in the promise that uncertainty can be converted into calculation. If enough information is collected, the reasoning goes, outcomes that once seemed unpredictable should become increasingly foreseeable. In many domains this approach is useful: forecasting demand, modelling weather patterns and estimating financial risk can all benefit from better data.

Yet better prediction does not necessarily produce better decisions. A prediction describes what is likely to happen; a decision requires a judgment about what ought to be done. The distinction becomes particularly important when the consequences of an action affect people whose preferences, incentives or behaviour may change in response to the prediction itself.

There is also a tendency to confuse precision with certainty. A forecast that assigns a probability of 73.4% may appear more authoritative than one that says “roughly three chances in four,” even when both communicate essentially the same uncertainty. Numerical precision can therefore create an impression of knowledge greater than the evidence warrants.

The appropriate response is not to abandon prediction. Rather, predictions should be treated as inputs into decisions, not substitutes for judgment. Their value lies not merely in how accurately they anticipate events but also in whether decision-makers understand the assumptions, uncertainty and consequences embedded in the forecast.

# RC 2 — WHY MAPS DISTORT

### Passage

A map is often judged by how closely it resembles the territory it represents. Yet no map can reproduce the world without selection. Roads are emphasised, buildings are omitted, borders are highlighted, and distances may be altered. These distortions are not necessarily failures; they are consequences of designing a representation for a particular purpose.

A map intended for a driver and one designed for a geologist may depict the same landscape very differently. The driver's map privileges routes and intersections, while the geologist's map may foreground rock formations and fault lines. Each sacrifices information that is less relevant to its intended use.

Problems arise when the conventions of one map are mistaken for features of the territory itself. A border drawn as a crisp line can encourage the impression that the physical world contains an equally crisp boundary, even when the boundary is historically or politically contested. Representation can therefore influence not only what people see but also how they conceptualise what they see.

The lesson is not that maps are deceptive. It is that representations should be evaluated in relation to their purpose. A useful representation is not the one that includes everything; it is one that makes the relevant features accessible while making its omissions and conventions sufficiently intelligible.
