### Passage

The concept of progress is often associated with accumulation: more knowledge, more technology, more production and greater control over the environment. Such measures are attractive because they are visible and relatively easy to compare across time. Yet accumulation alone does not tell us whether a society has become better at achieving the ends that its members value.

A society can become more technologically capable while becoming less capable of using that technology wisely. Similarly, an institution may acquire more information without improving the quality of its decisions. The distinction is therefore not between having resources and having none, but between possessing resources and being able to convert them into meaningful outcomes.

This does not imply that technological or economic growth is irrelevant. Resources expand the range of possible actions. But possibilities are not achievements. The ability to transform resources into desirable outcomes depends on institutions, incentives, skills and social norms.

The mistake, then, is to treat growth as evidence of progress without examining the mechanism connecting the two. Progress requires not merely an increase in what a society possesses, but an improvement in what it can accomplish with what it possesses.

### Passage

Scientific disagreement is sometimes portrayed as a failure of science. If two researchers interpret the same evidence differently, observers may conclude that the evidence is insufficient or that one researcher must be irrational. Yet disagreement can persist even within rigorous scientific practice because evidence rarely arrives with its interpretation already attached.

Researchers must decide which observations are relevant, which mechanisms are plausible and which uncertainties matter. Different background assumptions can therefore produce different hypotheses from the same evidence. This does not mean that all interpretations are equally valid. Evidence can rule out some explanations, and further experiments can narrow the range of plausible alternatives.

Indeed, disagreement can be productive when it identifies precisely which assumptions remain uncertain. Competing explanations create opportunities for experiments that discriminate between them. Scientific progress may therefore depend not on eliminating disagreement immediately, but on converting disagreement into testable questions.

\[
\frac d{60}+\frac d{40}=5

\[

\boxed C

**Source:** CAT-level inspired  
**Topic:** Time, Speed & Distance  
**Difficulty:** 4/5

⚡ **Shortcut:** Average speed for equal distances:

\[
\frac{2uv}{u+v}
=
\frac{2(60)(40)}{100}=48
\]

Total distance = \(5\times48=240\), so one-way = **120 km**.

❌ **Common trap:** Taking simple average \((60+40)/2=50\).

🧠 **Concept:** Equal-distance average speed is harmonic mean.

🔄 **Alternate:** Direct time equation as above.

### Today's most important shortcuts

**QA**
1. Equal-distance average speed → harmonic mean.
2. Pairwise work rates → add/subtract equations.
3. Equal SP + equal gain/loss → direct loss shortcut.
4. Root difference → \((a-b)^2=S^2-4P\).
5. Reciprocal powers → recurrence:
   \[
   S_n=(x+x^{-1})S_{n-1}-S_{n-2}.
   \]
6. Recognise Pythagorean triples: \(7-24-25\), \(6-8-10\).
7. Divisibility by \(9\) + “not 5” → subtract multiples of 45.
8. Circular arrangements → fix one person + gap method.
9. Repeated stopping probability → condition on the relevant outcomes.
10. Digit reversal:
   \[
   abc-cba=99(a-c).
   \]

### Your CAT mindset

At **99+ level**, the objective isn't:

> “Can I solve all 22 QA questions?”

It's:

> **“Can I identify the 8–10 questions that give me the highest expected return in the first 15–20 minutes?”**

The same applies to DILR: **set selection is part of the exam**, not an afterthought.

And because you've specifically asked for shortcut training, from this point the most valuable habit is:

**Concept → pattern recognition → shortcut → timed execution.**

Don't memorise a shortcut until you understand *why* it works. That is what lets you recognise variants of the same CAT problem under pressure.
