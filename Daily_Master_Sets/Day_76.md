# RC 1 — PRECISION AND REPRESENTATION

### Passage

A numerical indicator is attractive partly because it appears to transform a complicated reality into something that can be compared, ranked and communicated. This compression is not necessarily a weakness. Without some form of compression, many decisions would become practically impossible because the amount of information involved would exceed what people can reasonably process.

The difficulty arises when the convenience of the indicator is mistaken for completeness. Every indicator is constructed by selecting some features of a phenomenon and leaving others outside its immediate frame. The resulting number may therefore be entirely accurate as a measurement while still providing an incomplete account of the thing being measured.

This does not mean that indicators should be abandoned. It means that their interpretation must remain connected to their purpose. A measure designed to compare one dimension of a phenomenon should not quietly be treated as a complete description of the phenomenon itself. The question is not whether simplification occurs, but whether the simplification is appropriate to the task.

# RC 2 — INCENTIVES AND CHOICE

### Passage

People often describe incentives as though they operate mechanically: offer a reward and behaviour changes; remove the reward and behaviour returns to its previous state. Real-world responses are usually less predictable. An incentive enters an existing environment of expectations, habits and social meanings, and its effect depends partly on how people interpret the change.

Consider a workplace in which employees regularly help one another without being formally compensated for doing so. Introducing a small monetary reward for each act of assistance may increase some forms of cooperation, but it may also change the meaning employees attach to the activity. What was previously understood as collegial behaviour could begin to look like a transaction. The incentive has not merely altered the payoff; it may have altered the social interpretation of the behaviour.

This does not imply that incentives are ineffective. Rather, it suggests that their effects cannot always be predicted by looking only at their monetary value. Incentives interact with existing norms and motivations, and those interactions can produce outcomes that are larger, smaller or qualitatively different from what a simple calculation would suggest.
