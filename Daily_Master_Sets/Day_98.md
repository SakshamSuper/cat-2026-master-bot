# RC 1 — THE PARADOX OF STANDARDISATION

### Passage

Standardisation is frequently criticised for reducing diversity. When institutions adopt common procedures, identical measurements and uniform criteria, local variation can be suppressed. Yet standardisation also makes comparison possible. A measurement has little comparative value if every observer uses a different scale; similarly, a common procedure can allow institutions to identify differences that would otherwise be obscured by inconsistent methods.

The difficulty is therefore not standardisation itself but the purposes to which it is applied. A standard can establish a common baseline without dictating every aspect of practice. Problems arise when the baseline becomes a rigid template and variation is treated as error rather than as potentially useful adaptation.

The debate over standardisation consequently involves a trade-off. Some degree of uniformity is necessary for coordination and comparison, but excessive uniformity can eliminate information that emerges only from local experimentation. The question is not whether standards should exist, but how much variation a useful standard should permit.

# RC 2 — MEMORY AND HISTORY

### Passage

Collective memory is often described as a society's shared recollection of its past. But collective memory is not simply a large archive stored inside the minds of individuals. Communities continually select, reinterpret and sometimes contest what deserves to be remembered. Public monuments, school textbooks and commemorative ceremonies are therefore not merely records of the past; they are mechanisms through which particular interpretations of the past acquire visibility.

This does not imply that collective memory is necessarily false. A society may preserve historically accurate accounts while simultaneously choosing which events to emphasise and which to neglect. The important distinction is between the accuracy of a particular historical claim and the social process by which some claims become more prominent than others.

Understanding collective memory consequently requires examining both historical evidence and the institutions through which remembrance is organised. To study only whether a memory is factually correct is to overlook the question of why that memory, rather than another, occupies such a prominent place in public consciousness.
