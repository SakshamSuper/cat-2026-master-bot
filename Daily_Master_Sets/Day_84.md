# RC 1 — REPRESENTATION AND SIMPLIFICATION

### Passage

Every act of representation involves selection. A scientific model, a map, or even a statistical measure cannot reproduce everything about the phenomenon it describes. It must privilege some features and leave others in the background. This simplification is sometimes treated as a weakness, as though a representation were valuable only to the extent that it resembles reality in every detail.

But a representation need not maximise resemblance to be useful. A model of planetary motion may deliberately ignore countless physical details while preserving the relationships necessary to predict an orbit. A subway map may distort geographical distance while preserving the sequence and connectivity of stations. In both cases, what appears to be a departure from reality can be part of the representation's usefulness.

The relevant question, therefore, is not whether a representation eliminates simplification, but whether its simplifications preserve what matters for the task at hand. A representation that retains irrelevant detail may be less useful than one that discards it. Yet this does not mean that accuracy ceases to matter: a simplification becomes defective when it removes or distorts a relationship that the representation is supposed to preserve.

# RC 2 — METRICS AS PROXIES

### Passage

An organisation often needs to transform a broad objective into something measurable. The resulting metric provides a convenient way to compare performance, allocate rewards and identify apparent progress. Yet the convenience of measurement can create a conceptual error: the metric may gradually be treated as the objective itself.

Suppose an organisation wishes to improve the quality of customer service and therefore rewards employees according to the number of calls they handle. Employees who respond by increasing the number of calls handled are not necessarily behaving irrationally. They may simply be responding to the incentive they have been given. The organisation's mistake would be to assume that higher call volume necessarily indicates better service.

Metrics are therefore best understood as proxies. A proxy is useful only insofar as changes in it continue to correspond reasonably well with changes in the underlying objective. When that correspondence weakens, an organisation may observe improvement in the metric while making little progress toward what it actually values.

# 🔥 DAY 84 — 99+ PERCENTILE SHORTCUT BANK

### 1. Work problems
Use:

\[
\boxed{\text{Work}=\text{Rate}\times\text{Time}}
\]

and choose an LCM work-unit whenever individual completion times are involved.

---

### 2. Successive percentages

Never add percentage changes.

For a 40% mark-up followed by 20% discount:

\[
1.4\times0.8=1.12.
\]

So the net result is **12% increase**, not 20%.

---

### 3. Symmetric algebra

If roots are \(x,y\), immediately write:

\[
x+y,\quad xy.
\]

For example:

\[
x^3+y^3=(x+y)^3-3xy(x+y).
\]

This is much faster than solving the quadratic.

---

### 4. Chord geometry

The moment you see:

> chord + distance from centre

think:

\[
\boxed{\text{half chord}^2+\text{distance}^2=r^2}.
\]

Look for Pythagorean triples.

---

### 5. Divisor restrictions

For:

\[
N=p^aq^br^c,
\]

ordinary divisors:

\[
(a+1)(b+1)(c+1).
\]

But if a divisor must be divisible by \(p^kq^l\), the exponent ranges become:

\[
k,\ldots,a
\]

and

\[
l,\ldots,b.
\]

This is much faster than listing divisors.

---

### 6. Permutations with zero

When 0 is available and the number cannot begin with 0:

> **Handle the leading position separately.**

This is one of the most common CAT permutation traps.

---

### 7. Probability

For conditions such as:

> product is divisible by 6

think in terms of prime factors:

\[
6=2\times3.
\]

Then ask:

> Where can the factor 2 come from?  
> Where can the factor 3 come from?

This is usually faster than checking 36 outcomes individually.
