# RC 1 — THE VALUE OF ABSTRACTION

### Passage

Abstraction is sometimes criticised for removing the complexity that gives a phenomenon its meaning. Such criticism is justified when abstraction becomes an excuse for ignoring relevant differences. Yet abstraction is also indispensable to thought: without selecting certain features and temporarily setting others aside, it would be difficult to compare cases, identify patterns or formulate general explanations.

The real difficulty, therefore, is not abstraction itself but the failure to recognise what an abstraction has excluded. A map, for example, can be useful precisely because it omits countless details of a landscape. A road map and a geological map omit different things because they answer different questions. Neither becomes defective merely because it fails to represent what the other represents.

Problems emerge when an abstraction designed for one purpose is treated as if it were a complete account of the phenomenon. The simplification then ceases to function as a tool and becomes a source of distortion. The solution is not to abandon abstraction but to maintain awareness of the relationship between what is represented, what is omitted and what question the representation was intended to answer.

# RC 2 — DEFAULTS AND HUMAN CHOICE

### Passage

A default option is often understood as merely a convenient starting point: if people do nothing, the default determines what happens. This description, however, understates the behavioural significance of defaults. Choosing an alternative may require attention, effort or information that the default does not require. Consequently, two options can be formally available while being practically unequal in accessibility.

This does not imply that defaults eliminate choice. Individuals can actively reject them, and many do. But the ability to resist a default does not make the default irrelevant. A person's decision is made within an environment in which some actions require less effort than others. The resulting differences in effort can matter even when individuals retain complete formal freedom.

The significance of defaults is therefore best understood through the interaction between individual agency and choice architecture. A default does not force a decision; rather, it changes the conditions under which the decision is made. This distinction is important because it avoids two opposite mistakes: assuming that people are entirely autonomous decision-makers unaffected by context, or assuming that contextual influences leave them without meaningful agency.
