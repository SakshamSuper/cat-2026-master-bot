### Passage

The appeal of efficiency is difficult to resist. Organisations routinely seek ways to complete tasks faster, reduce costs and eliminate unnecessary effort. Yet efficiency is meaningful only relative to a purpose. A process can become extremely efficient at producing an outcome that nobody actually values.

This problem becomes visible when institutions rely heavily on measurable indicators. Once a target is quantified, employees may rationally devote their energy to improving the indicator rather than the broader objective it was intended to represent. The result is not necessarily dishonesty or incompetence; it may simply be a consequence of incentives.

Consider a customer-support organisation that evaluates employees almost entirely by the number of calls completed per hour. Employees who spend additional time resolving difficult problems may appear less productive than employees who rapidly close simple calls. The metric has not necessarily measured performance incorrectly; rather, it has measured only one dimension of performance.

The solution is not to abandon measurement. Instead, institutions need to distinguish between what is easy to measure and what is important to achieve. Sometimes the most valuable outcomes are precisely those that are difficult to quantify.

### Passage

When people discuss technological disruption, they often focus on the technology itself: its capabilities, speed and novelty. But technologies do not enter empty environments. They enter systems composed of regulations, skills, infrastructure, habits and existing business models. These surrounding structures influence not only how quickly a technology spreads but also what effects it ultimately produces.

The history of electricity illustrates this point. The existence of electrical power did not instantly transform every factory. Production systems had to be reorganised so that machines could exploit the flexibility of distributed power. Workers and managers had to adapt, and complementary technologies developed around electricity.

The same principle complicates predictions about artificial intelligence. A highly capable model may perform a task in a laboratory, but its economic impact depends on whether organisations can integrate it into actual workflows. Adoption may require new data systems, revised responsibilities, employee training and changes in regulation.

Thus, technological capability is an important variable, but it is not a complete forecast of technological impact. Understanding disruption requires studying the ecosystem into which an innovation is introduced.

## Q1 — **B, 18 days**

Take total work = 72 units.

A's rate:

\[
72/18=4
\]

B's rate:

\[
72/24=3
\]

Together for 8 days:

\[
(4+3)\times8=56
\]

Remaining:

\[
72-56=16
\]

B + C finish this in 3 days:

\[
B+C=\frac{16}{3}
\]

B's rate = 3, therefore:

\[
C=\frac{16}{3}-3=\frac73
\]

C alone for entire work:

\[
\frac{72}{7/3}=\frac{216}{7}
\]

This is approximately 30.86 days, so **the options are inconsistent**.

### Important CAT lesson
Do **not** bend your mathematics to match an option. The calculated answer is:

\[
\boxed{\frac{216}{7}\text{ days}}
\]

**Source:** CAT-level inspired  
**Topic:** Time & Work  
**Difficulty:** 4/5  
**Recommended time:** 2.5 min  
**⚡ Shortcut:** Use total work as LCM(18,24)=72.  
**❌ Trap:** Treating “remaining work” as a fixed number of days.  
**🧠 Concept:** Work rate = reciprocal of time.  
**Alternate:** Direct rate equations.

- A,C ≠ 2

Possible valid schedules are:

1. A C F B D E
2. A C F E B D

4. A E F C B D
5. B D F A C E

### Q13 — **A**

In every valid arrangement, either:

- A is first, or
- B is first.

But is A **always** first? No — arrangements 5 and 6 have B first.

So the answer isn't A.

Let's inspect the options:

- A: A slot 1 → not always.
- B: B slot 1 → not always.
- C: E after D → not always.
- D: C slot 6 → not always.

Therefore **none of the options is universally true**.

This is a set-quality issue. The underlying correct invariant is:

\[
\boxed{F=3}

# 🎯 CAT Mentor Analysis for Today

### High-value shortcuts to retain

**QA**
- Pairwise work rates → use LCM/work-unit method.
- Repeated replacement → remaining fraction raised to the number of operations.
- Successive discounts → multiply factors, never add percentages.
- Quadratic roots → use \(S^2-4P\).
- Reciprocal expressions → build powers sequentially.
- \(13,14,15\) triangle → area \(84\).
- Common-base triangles → area ratio = height ratio.
- Modular arithmetic → find short cycles.
- Exactly-one divisibility → \(S_1-2S_2+3S_3\).
- Circular seating → fix one person, then use gaps.

**DILR**
- Translate percentages into actual quantities immediately.
- Calculate **only the change** when one parameter changes.
- For ordering sets, build forced blocks before cases.
- Scan a DILR set before committing 15–20 minutes.

**VARC**
- Main idea must cover the **whole passage**, including qualifications.
- Reject options containing unsupported absolutes.
- In Para Jumbles, track **contrast words, pronouns, demonstratives and conclusions**.
- In Sentence Placement, “this/that/therefore/however” are powerful anchors.
