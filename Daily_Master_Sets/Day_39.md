# RC 1

### Passage

Efficiency is frequently treated as an unquestionable virtue. An organisation that uses fewer resources to produce the same output appears superior to one that uses more. Yet efficiency is meaningful only when the output being optimised is itself desirable. A system can become extremely efficient at producing an outcome that society does not value.

The difficulty becomes especially visible when institutions use numerical targets. Once a target is established, individuals have an incentive to improve the target itself. This may be perfectly rational behaviour rather than deliberate manipulation. If a hospital is rewarded primarily for reducing the average duration of patient stays, for instance, reducing stays may become more important than ensuring that patients receive adequate care.

The lesson is not that targets or efficiency measures should be abandoned. Rather, measurement systems must be designed with awareness of the distinction between an indicator and the objective it represents. Good systems therefore require multiple indicators and periodic examination of whether the indicators continue to reflect the underlying purpose.

# RC 2

### Passage

A common misunderstanding of innovation is that useful inventions succeed simply because they are technologically superior. In reality, adoption depends on a network of complementary conditions. Users must understand the technology, organisations must be able to integrate it, infrastructure may need to change, and economic incentives must make adoption worthwhile.

This helps explain why technically promising inventions sometimes fail to spread. A technology may perform well in controlled conditions but impose substantial costs when introduced into an existing system. Conversely, a less sophisticated technology may spread rapidly if it fits existing habits and infrastructure.

The history of innovation therefore suggests that technological performance is only one component of success. The broader environment determines whether an invention can move from technical possibility to practical use.

# 🎯 Today's 99+ Shortcut Bank

### QA
- Average speed = total distance ÷ total time.
- Equal-distance speeds → harmonic mean.
- Work questions → choose a convenient total work such as LCM.
- Mixture replacement → track the component whose final quantity is easiest to determine.
- Quadratic:
  \[
  (a-b)^2=(a+b)^2-4ab.
  \]
- Reciprocal identity:
  \[
  x^3+x^{-3}=a^3-3a.
  \]
- Right-triangle inradius:
  \[
  r=\frac{a+b-c}{2}.
  \]
- Chord:
  \[
  \left(\frac{\text{chord}}2\right)^2+d^2=r^2.
  \]
- Modular arithmetic → hunt for small cycles.
- “A but not B” → subtract the intersection.
- Divisibility by 5 → last digit fixed.
- Simultaneous card selection → combinations.

### DILR
For ranking problems, build **chains** rather than individual comparisons.

For DI percentage-change questions:

\[
\boxed{\text{Change in total}=\text{change in affected component only}}
\]

This is one of the highest-value CAT DI shortcuts.

### VARC
For RC:
1. Identify thesis.
2. Identify author's qualification.
3. Separate example from argument.
4. For inference, choose the **minimum claim supported by the passage**.

For VA:
- **However / but / yet** → contrast.
- **Therefore / consequently** → conclusion.
- **This / these / such** → backward reference.
- A sentence explaining a pronoun/reference usually cannot come before its antecedent.

### 99+ rule for today

Don't measure yourself only by raw accuracy.

For each question you miss, classify it:

**C = Concept gap**  
**S = Selection error**  
**T = Time-management error**  
**A = Arithmetic/calculation error**  
**R = Reasoning error**

For a 99+ percentile CAT attempt, **S and T are often as important as C**. Your goal is to become someone who can look at a hard question and decide within ~20–30 seconds whether it deserves your time.
