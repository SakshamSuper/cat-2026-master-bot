### Passage

Measurement appears to offer an attractive solution to difficult decisions. Once a phenomenon is converted into a number, comparisons become easier, progress can be tracked and goals can be communicated. Yet the convenience of measurement can create a subtle danger: what is measurable may gradually become a substitute for what is actually valuable.

This does not mean that numerical indicators are inherently misleading. Rather, their usefulness depends on the relationship between the indicator and the objective it represents. If a school uses examination scores as one measure of learning, the indicator may be informative. But if schools are rewarded almost exclusively for increasing scores, teaching may become narrowly focused on what appears in the examination.

The problem therefore lies less in measurement itself than in the incentives surrounding it. A metric can influence behaviour because people respond rationally to the rewards attached to it. Consequently, a system may improve its measured performance while simultaneously moving away from its broader purpose.

Good measurement systems must therefore recognise that indicators are representations, not the objectives themselves. Multiple measures, periodic review and awareness of unintended incentives can reduce—but never completely eliminate—the risk.

### Passage

People often imagine that scientific progress occurs when one theory is replaced by another. While this sometimes happens, scientific change is usually less dramatic. Older theories may remain useful within limited domains even after a more general theory has been developed.

Newtonian mechanics provides a familiar example. Relativity did not make Newton's equations useless for ordinary speeds and everyday scales. Instead, it showed the conditions under which those equations provide an excellent approximation and the circumstances in which a more general framework is required.

This illustrates an important feature of scientific knowledge: usefulness and ultimate generality are not identical. A model can be extremely effective for a particular purpose without being universally applicable. Scientific progress therefore often involves identifying the boundaries of a model's validity rather than simply declaring the model true or false.

The history of science is consequently better understood as an expansion and refinement of explanatory frameworks than as a simple sequence in which old knowledge is discarded whenever new knowledge appears.

## Q1 — **C, 30 km**

Let one-way distance = \(d\).

Actual total time:

\[
\frac d{40}+\frac d{60}
=
\frac{5d}{120}
=
\frac d{24}
\]

At 50 km/h both ways:

\[
\frac{2d}{50}=\frac d{25}
\]

Difference is 24 minutes:

\[
\frac d{24}-\frac d{25}=\frac{24}{60}=\frac25
\]

\[
d\left(\frac1{600}\right)=\frac25
\]

\[
d=240
\]

So the correct distance is actually **240 km**, which is not among the options.

### Important
The numerical setup is internally valid, but the answer choices are not.

\[
\boxed{240\text{ km}}
\]

**Source:** CAT-level inspired  
**Topic:** Time, Speed & Distance  
**Difficulty:** 4/5  
**Time:** 2.5 min  

⚡ **Shortcut:** Equal-distance average speed:

\[
\frac{2(40)(60)}{40+60}=48
\]

Actual total time = \(2d/48=d/24\).  
At 50 both ways = \(d/25\).

Difference:

\[
d\left(\frac1{24}-\frac1{25}\right)=0.4
\]

❌ **Trap:** Using arithmetic mean speed = 50.

🧠 **Concept:** Equal-distance average speed is harmonic mean.
