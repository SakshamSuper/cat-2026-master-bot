### Passage

Numbers appear to make complex decisions objective. A numerical indicator allows organisations to compare performance, establish targets and evaluate change over time. Yet the appearance of objectivity can conceal an important problem: the number is a representation of reality, not reality itself.

Once an indicator becomes the basis for rewards, people naturally begin to respond to it. This response need not involve dishonesty. Individuals may simply redirect their effort toward what the system rewards. Consequently, an organisation can become increasingly successful according to its chosen metric while becoming less successful according to its underlying purpose.

The solution is not to reject measurement. Without measurement, many decisions become difficult to evaluate. Instead, measurement systems need to distinguish between indicators and objectives. Multiple measures, periodic review and attention to unintended incentives can make indicators more useful.

The fundamental challenge is therefore not whether to measure, but how to prevent the measurement from becoming a substitute for the objective it was intended to represent.

Innovation is often described as a contest in which the technically best invention eventually wins. This view, however, overlooks the environment in which technologies operate. An invention must fit existing infrastructure, organisational capabilities, user behaviour and economic incentives if it is to spread widely.

## Q1 — **C, 50 km/h**

First 120 km:

\[
t_1=\frac{120}{40}=3
\]

Next 180 km:

\[
t_2=\frac{180}{60}=3
\]

Total:

\[
300\text{ km in }6\text{ hours}
\]

\[
\boxed{50\text{ km/h}}
\]

**Source:** CAT-level inspired  
**Topic:** TSD  
**Difficulty:** 3/5  
**Time:** 2 min  

⚡ **Shortcut:** Never average speeds directly unless distances are equal. Here simply use total distance/total time.

❌ **Trap:** Taking \((40+60)/2=50\) happens to give the right answer here, but only by coincidence because the two distances happen to produce equal times.

🧠 **Revision:** Average speed = total distance ÷ total time.

# 🔥 DAY 40 — 99+ SHORTCUT BANK

### QA shortcuts to retain

**1. Average speed**

Never average speeds blindly.

\[
\boxed{\text{Average speed}=\frac{\text{total distance}}{\text{total time}}}
\]

**2. Work**

Use LCM as total work whenever possible.

**3. Mixtures**

If only one component matters, track that component instead of writing the entire mixture equation.

**4. Quadratics**

If root sum \(S\) and root difference \(D\) are known:

\[
\boxed{P=\frac{S^2-D^2}{4}}
\]

**5. Reciprocal expressions**

If

\[
x+\frac1x=a
\]

then:

\[
x^2+\frac1{x^2}=a^2-2
\]

and

\[
x^4+\frac1{x^4}=(a^2-2)^2-2.
\]

**6. Circle chord**

\[
\boxed{\text{half chord}=\sqrt{r^2-d^2}}
\]

**7. Divisibility**

For:

> divisible by A but not B

think:

\[
\boxed{A-(A\cap B)}
\]

**8. P&C**

A divisibility condition often fixes one digit/position. **Fix it first**, then arrange the rest.
