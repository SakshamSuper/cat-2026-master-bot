### Passage

A useful distinction in evaluating institutions is the distinction between rules and outcomes. Rules are designed to guide behaviour before an outcome occurs; outcomes are what actually result from that behaviour. It is tempting to judge a rule solely by whether a particular outcome was good or bad. Yet such a judgment can be misleading because the same rule may produce different outcomes under different circumstances.

This does not make outcomes irrelevant. Institutions ultimately exist to produce certain desirable results. But evaluating institutions only through outcomes can encourage retrospective reasoning: once an outcome is known, people may judge the decision that produced it as though the decision-maker had possessed information that was unavailable at the time.

A more useful evaluation therefore considers both the quality of the decision process and the outcome. A poor process can occasionally produce a good result by luck, while a sound process can occasionally produce a bad result because of factors outside the decision-maker's control.

### Passage

Scientific models are often criticised because they simplify reality. Such criticism assumes that the purpose of a model is to reproduce the world in complete detail. But simplification is not necessarily a defect. A model is useful precisely because it excludes certain details and focuses attention on relationships relevant to a particular question.

Different models may therefore represent the same phenomenon differently without one of them necessarily being wrong. A model designed to predict short-term weather patterns may require different features from one designed to explain long-term climate trends. The appropriate model depends partly on the question the researcher is trying to answer.

The real danger lies not in simplification itself but in forgetting what has been simplified away. A model becomes misleading when its users mistake a selective representation for a complete description.
