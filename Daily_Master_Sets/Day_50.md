### Passage

Modern institutions often treat prediction as a solution to uncertainty. If future events can be forecast accurately, the reasoning goes, decisions can be made with greater confidence. Yet prediction and understanding are not identical. A model may successfully predict an event without revealing the mechanisms that produce it, just as a person may recognise a pattern without knowing why the pattern exists.

This distinction matters because prediction can be highly dependent on the conditions under which a model was developed. When those conditions change, a model that once performed well may become unreliable. Understanding mechanisms, by contrast, can sometimes allow decision-makers to adapt when circumstances change.

The value of prediction should therefore not be dismissed. Rather, prediction is most useful when its limits are understood and when it is supplemented, where necessary, by an understanding of the processes being predicted.

### Passage

Historical narratives frequently organise complex changes around decisive events: a revolution, a technological breakthrough, a political reform or an economic crisis. Such events are useful anchors because they provide a clear point around which a story can be constructed. But the clarity of the narrative can create an illusion of simplicity. Large transformations are often prepared by gradual changes that receive less attention because they lack a dramatic beginning.

This does not make turning points irrelevant. A political reform may genuinely alter the direction of a society, and a technological breakthrough may dramatically expand what is possible. The point is rather that turning points operate within conditions that have often been developing for years or decades.

A fuller understanding of historical change therefore requires attention both to visible events and to the slower processes that make those events consequential.

# Q12 — **16**

We need:

\[
x+y=50
\]

and:

\[
12\mid xy.
\]

Since:

\[
12=3\times4,
\]

we need \(xy\) divisible by both 3 and 4.

Because \(x+y=50\), modulo 3:

\[
x+y\equiv2\pmod3.
\]

Thus \(x\) and \(y\) cannot both be nonmultiples of 3 in the combinations that fail the product condition; more directly, test divisibility patterns systematically.

For positive \(x<y\), the valid pairs are:

\[
(2,48),(6,44),(8,42),(12,38),
\]

\[
(14,36),(18,32),(20,30),(24,26).
\]

Each has \(xy\) divisible by 12.

Their reverses are also valid:

\[
(48,2),(44,6),\ldots,(26,24).
\]

Hence:

\[
8\times2
=
\boxed{16}.
\]

**Source:** CAT-level inspired  
**Topic:** Number theory / divisibility  
**Difficulty:** 5/5  
**Recommended time:** 4 min  

**Shortcut:** Because \(x+y=50\) is even, \(x,y\) have the same parity. Separate:

- both even;
- both odd.

An odd × odd product cannot be divisible by 4, so both must be even.

Set:

\[
x=2a,\quad y=2b.
\]

Then:

\[
a+b=25
\]

and we require \(ab\) divisible by 3.

Since 25 is not divisible by 3, exactly one of \(a,b\) must be divisible by 3.

There are 8 choices for the smaller \(a\), giving 8 unordered pairs and hence 16 ordered pairs.

**Common trap:** Forgetting the factor 4 in 12 and checking only divisibility by 3.

**Key concept:** Exploit parity before checking divisibility.

**Alternate method:** Work directly modulo 12 across \(x=1,\ldots,49\), using symmetry \(x\leftrightarrow50-x\).
