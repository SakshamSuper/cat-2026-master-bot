# RC 1 — CATEGORIES AND PURPOSE

### Passage

Classification is often treated as a neutral act of placing objects into groups according to properties that those objects simply possess. Yet classification does more than record similarities. It decides which similarities are relevant and which differences may be ignored for a particular purpose. The same objects can therefore be organised into different categories without any of those classifications being necessarily mistaken.

This does not mean that categories are arbitrary. A classification must still respond to features that matter to the task for which it is designed. A classification intended to predict behaviour, for example, may group objects differently from one intended to explain their historical origins. The difference between the two classifications is not necessarily a disagreement about facts; it may instead reflect different questions.

The mistake, therefore, is to assume that one classification must reveal the single meaningful structure of reality. Categories are instruments of inquiry. Their usefulness depends partly on what they are intended to accomplish.

# RC 2 — METRICS AND OBJECTIVES

### Passage

Organisations often use measurable targets because numbers make performance easier to compare. Yet a metric is only an indicator of an objective; it is not necessarily the objective itself. Once a metric becomes the focus of incentives, people may discover ways to improve the measured quantity without improving the broader outcome that the quantity was meant to represent.

This behaviour need not arise from dishonesty or irrationality. If employees are rewarded for achieving a particular target, responding to that target may be entirely rational even when the organisation later discovers that the target was an imperfect proxy for its real goal. The problem therefore lies not simply in individual behaviour but in the relationship between the metric and the objective.

This does not mean that organisations should abandon measurement. Rather, they must periodically examine whether the indicators they optimise still correspond to the purposes they are intended to serve. A metric is useful precisely because it represents something beyond itself; when that connection is forgotten, optimisation can become counterproductive.

# 🔥 DAY 80 — 99+ PERCENTILE SHORTCUT BANK

### 1. Time & Work
Don't work with fractions when the numbers are clean.

For 12 and 24 days:

\[
\boxed{\text{LCM}=24}
\]

turns rates into 2 and 1.

### 2. SI-CI
For exactly two years:

\[
\boxed{CI-SI=P\left(\frac r{100}\right)^2}
\]

This should become an **instant-recognition formula**.

### 3. Profit + Discount

Never add percentages blindly.

If:

\[
CP=100,
\]

profit 8% gives 108, while 10% discount means 108 is 90% of MP.

### 4. Algebra

Whenever you see:

\[
x+\frac1x,
\]

immediately think:

\[
\boxed{x^2+\frac1{x^2}=S^2-2}.
\]

### 5. Modulus

\[
\boxed{|A|=|B|\Rightarrow A=B\text{ or }A=-B}
\]

This is much faster than squaring.

### 6. Coordinate Geometry

Right angle at a point?

Think:

\[
\boxed{\vec a\cdot\vec b=0}
\]

before calculating distances.

### 7. Factorials

For trailing zero/divisibility by powers of 10:

\[
\boxed{\text{count powers of 5}}
\]

because powers of 2 are usually abundant.

### 8. DILR

Today's biggest lesson:

> **Before counting, check whether a condition immediately kills the case.**

Q16 is intentionally a 10-second question. A 99+ percentile candidate should not spend 90 seconds on it.

### 9. VARC

The two RCs deliberately test a recurring CAT skill:

\[
\boxed{\text{qualified argument}\neq\text{extreme argument}}
\]

Watch for words such as:

- necessarily
- always
- only
- completely
- inherently

They frequently make an otherwise attractive option incorrect.
