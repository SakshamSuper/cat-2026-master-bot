# RC 1 — THE COST OF PRECISION

### Passage

Precision is generally treated as an unquestionable virtue. A measurement reported to several decimal places appears more scientific than one reported roughly, and a model with many parameters may seem more sophisticated than a simpler one. Yet precision and accuracy are not synonymous. A measurement can be extremely precise while being consistently wrong, just as a highly elaborate model can reproduce details without representing the underlying phenomenon well.

The attraction of precision partly arises from its visibility. What can be expressed numerically and finely differentiated is easier to display, compare and defend. Uncertainty, by contrast, is harder to communicate because it resists neat boundaries. Institutions may therefore reward apparent precision even when acknowledging uncertainty would produce a more honest representation of what is known.

This does not mean that precision is undesirable. Where the measurement process is reliable and the distinction between nearby values matters, greater precision is genuinely useful. The problem occurs when precision becomes a substitute for understanding. In such cases, adding decimal places or variables can create the appearance of knowledge without increasing its substance.

# RC 2 — TECHNOLOGY AND SKILL

### Passage

When technology automates a task, observers often ask whether the technology will replace the people who previously performed it. This framing is incomplete. Automation can indeed eliminate particular activities, but it can simultaneously increase the value of other activities that were previously less visible. A navigation system, for example, may reduce the need to memorise routes while increasing the importance of deciding where one actually wants to go.

The effect of automation therefore depends partly on how work is reorganised around it. If workers merely surrender a task to a machine, their role may shrink. If the technology frees them from routine operations and allows them to devote greater attention to interpretation, judgement or coordination, their role may change rather than disappear.

This is why technological change cannot be understood simply by counting which tasks machines can perform. One must also examine the complementary capabilities that become more valuable when those tasks are automated. The important question is not merely “What can the machine do?” but “What becomes more important because the machine can do this?”
