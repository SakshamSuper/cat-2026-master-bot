# RC 1 — CLASSIFICATION AND REALITY

### Passage

A classification system is sometimes judged by asking whether it accurately reflects the objects it divides into categories. Such a test is reasonable, but it can obscure another question: accurate for what purpose? A classification designed to predict future behaviour may organise objects differently from one designed to reconstruct their historical development. The difference does not necessarily mean that one classification captures reality while the other distorts it.

This is because categories do not simply discover distinctions that exist independently of human purposes. They also make some distinctions salient and render others comparatively unimportant. That does not make classifications arbitrary. A useful classification must still respond to relevant features of the objects being studied. But relevance itself depends partly on the problem the classification is intended to solve.

Consequently, disagreement between two classification systems should not automatically be interpreted as disagreement about facts. Sometimes the disagreement is about which features deserve priority for a particular purpose. Treating one classification as universally authoritative may therefore confuse usefulness with exclusivity.

# RC 2 — INCENTIVES AND METRICS

### Passage

An organisation may introduce a metric because it wants to make a broad objective measurable. The metric can then become useful for comparing performance across individuals or departments. Yet the convenience of measurement creates a potential danger: once rewards are attached to the metric, the metric can become the immediate object of optimisation rather than a proxy for the broader objective.

This does not imply that employees who respond to such incentives are behaving irrationally. If the organisation explicitly rewards a particular outcome, pursuing that outcome can be entirely rational from the employee's perspective. The difficulty arises when the organisation mistakes success on the indicator for success on the objective.

For this reason, a metric should be treated as an instrument rather than an unquestionable definition of success. Organisations need to examine periodically whether the indicator still tracks what they actually care about. Measurement is valuable, but its value depends on preserving the connection between the number being optimised and the purpose that number was introduced to represent.

Let the one-way distance be \(D\).

Total distance:

\[
2D.

Total time:

\[
\frac D{60}+\frac D{40}
\]

\[
=\frac{2D+3D}{120}
=\frac{5D}{120}
=\frac D{24}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\text{Average speed}
=
\frac{2D}{D/24}
=48.
\]

\[
\boxed{48\text{ km/h}}

**Answer: B**

**Source:** CAT-level inspired  
**Topic:** Time, Speed & Distance  
**Difficulty:** 4/5  
**Recommended time:** 1.5 min

\[
\boxed{\text{Average speed}=\frac{2uv}{u+v}}
\]
so:
\[
\frac{2(60)(40)}{100}=48.
\]
**Common trap:** Taking the arithmetic mean:
\[
(60+40)/2=50.
\]
**Key concept:** Average speed = total distance / total time, not average of speeds unless times are equal.  
**Alternate:** Use the harmonic mean formula above.
