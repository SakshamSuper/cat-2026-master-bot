# RC 1 — EXPERTISE AND SELECTIVITY

### Passage

Expertise is often mistaken for the possession of a large quantity of information. Yet experts distinguish themselves not merely by knowing more, but by knowing which pieces of information matter in a particular situation. A chess master does not evaluate every possible feature of a board with equal attention; experience allows the player to recognise patterns and discard irrelevant possibilities.

This selectivity can appear paradoxical. The novice may believe that careful reasoning requires considering every available fact, whereas the expert may reach a conclusion after noticing only a few features. But the apparent speed of the expert is often the result of a deeper internal structure: years of experience have made certain distinctions almost automatic.

This does not mean expertise is infallible. A person who has become too accustomed to familiar patterns may fail when circumstances change. Expertise therefore combines efficient recognition with the ability to recognise when a familiar pattern no longer applies.

# RC 2 — TECHNOLOGY AND INSTITUTIONS

### Passage

Technological change is frequently described as if it automatically produces institutional change. A new communication technology, for example, may make information available more quickly, but this does not by itself determine how organisations will use that information. Existing rules, incentives and habits can mediate the effect of the technology.

This helps explain why similar technologies sometimes produce very different outcomes in different societies or organisations. A digital platform introduced into one institution may decentralise decision-making, while the same platform elsewhere may reinforce existing hierarchies. The technology creates possibilities, but institutions determine which possibilities become routine.

Consequently, technological capability should not be confused with technological impact. Understanding the latter requires attention not only to what a technology can do, but also to the institutional environment into which it is introduced.
