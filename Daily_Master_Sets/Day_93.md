# RC 1 — THE VALUE OF SLOWER INQUIRY

### Passage

Modern institutions often praise speed because rapid decisions appear to signal efficiency. Yet speed is valuable only when the process being accelerated is itself appropriate. A quick answer to a poorly framed question is not necessarily more useful than a slower answer that first examines whether the question deserves to be asked in its original form.

This does not imply that deliberation is always superior. In situations where the relevant variables are well understood and consequences are reversible, rapid decisions may indeed be advantageous. The problem arises when institutions generalise this logic and treat delay itself as evidence of incompetence.

The deeper issue, therefore, is not whether decisions should be fast or slow. It is whether the time devoted to a decision is proportionate to the uncertainty, irreversibility and complexity involved. Efficiency should be measured not simply by how quickly an institution reaches a decision, but by whether it allocates attention appropriately before reaching one.

# RC 2 — CATEGORIES AND PERCEPTION

### Passage

Categories are indispensable to thought because they allow people to compress a vast amount of information into manageable distinctions. To recognise something as a “tree,” for instance, is to avoid analysing every leaf, branch and cell independently each time one encounters one. Categories thus make perception and reasoning economical.

But categories are not neutral containers waiting to be filled with facts. The boundaries of a category can influence what people notice and what they overlook. Once a distinction becomes habitual, features that do not fit the distinction may receive less attention even when they are significant.

This creates a paradox. Categories make thought possible at scale precisely because they simplify reality, yet that simplification can become a source of distortion. The solution is not to abandon categories—which would make organised thought impossible—but to recognise when a useful distinction has ceased to be useful in the context at hand.

## Q1 — **C**

Let initial quantity be \(x\) litres.

Initially:

\[
\text{Milk}=\frac58x,\qquad
\text{Water}=\frac38x.
\]

When 16 L is removed, milk removed:

\[
16\cdot\frac58=10
\]

and water removed:

\[
16\cdot\frac38=6.
\]

So remaining milk:

\[
\frac58x-10.
\]

Remaining water:

\[
\frac38x-6.
\]

After adding 16 L water:

\[
\text{Water}=\frac38x+10.
\]

Given new ratio \(5:7\):

\[
\frac{\frac58x-10}{\frac38x+10}=\frac57.
\]

Cross multiply:

\[
7\left(\frac58x-10\right)
=
5\left(\frac38x+10\right).
\]

\[
\frac{35x}{8}-70
=
\frac{15x}{8}+50.
\]

\[
\frac{20x}{8}=120
\]

\[
x=48.
\]

\[
\boxed{48\text{ L}}
\]

**Answer: C**

**Source:** CAT-level inspired  
**Topic:** Mixtures / Replacement  
**Difficulty:** 4/5  
**Recommended time:** 1.5 min  
**Shortcut:** Track the amount of milk only—the replacement is pure water, so milk simply falls from \(5x/8\) to \(5x/8-10\).  
**Common trap:** Assuming the 16 L removed contains 16 L of milk.  
**Key concept revision:** In replacement problems, removed liquid has the **same composition as the original mixture**.  
**Alternate method:** Use the standard replacement formula for the fraction of original milk remaining.

## Q12 — **C**

We have:

\[
x+y+z=30
\]

and:

\[
xy+yz+zx=299.
\]

Use:

\[
(x+y+z)^2=x^2+y^2+z^2+2(xy+yz+zx).
\]

Therefore:

\[
900=x^2+y^2+z^2+598
\]

so:

\[
x^2+y^2+z^2=302.
\]

Now use:

\[
(x-y)^2+(y-z)^2+(z-x)^2
\]

\[
=2(x^2+y^2+z^2-xy-yz-zx)
\]

\[
=2(302-299)=6.
\]

Thus:

\[
(x-y)^2+(y-z)^2+(z-x)^2=6.
\]

Since \(x,y,z\) are integers and \(x\le y\le z\), the only possibility is:

\[
x,y,z=14,15,1?
\]

That cannot sum to 30. So let's inspect the integer structure carefully.

The squared differences must sum to 6. The only nonzero integer square possibility is:

\[
1+1+4=6.
\]

Thus the three pairwise differences must be \(1,1,2\).

Hence the numbers are three consecutive integers:

\[
14,15,16.
\]

Their sum is:

\[
45,

# TODAY'S 99+ PERCENTILE TAKEAWAYS

### 1. Don't confuse “hard” with “long”

Q4, Q5, Q8 and Q9 are deliberately short once the correct concept is recognised.

At 99+ level:

\[
\boxed{\text{Concept recognition}>\text{calculation}}
\]

### 2. Use structural identities in difficult QA

For symmetric integer questions, remember:

\[
(x+y+z)^2=x^2+y^2+z^2+2(xy+yz+zx)
\]

and

\[
(x-y)^2+(y-z)^2+(z-x)^2
=
2[(x+y+z)^2-3(xy+yz+zx)].
\]

This can turn a seemingly complicated integer problem into a tiny difference analysis.

### 3. DILR selection remains crucial

Today's Set 1 contains a very useful CAT habit:

> **Exploit deterministic constraints before constructing cases.**

For example, in Q16:

\[
S=3\Rightarrow R=4
\]

and the question is finished in seconds.

### 4. VARC — qualification is everything

The hardest options today are designed around:

- **always** vs **sometimes**
- **must** vs **can**
- **abandon** vs **reconsider**
- **speed is bad** vs **speed isn't automatically efficient**

For 99+ VARC, don't merely ask *“Does this option sound reasonable?”*

Ask:

\[
\boxed{\text{“Is this exactly as strong as the author's claim?”}}
\]
