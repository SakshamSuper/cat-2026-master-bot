# RC 1 — CLASSIFICATION AND PURPOSE

### Passage

A map is often mistaken for a miniature version of the territory it represents. Yet every map necessarily leaves things out. Roads may be emphasised while geological features are ignored; political boundaries may be highlighted while distances are distorted. Such omissions do not automatically make a map inaccurate. They may make it more useful for the particular task for which it was designed.

The same principle applies to many systems of representation. A representation becomes useful by selecting certain features and suppressing others. What counts as an important feature therefore depends partly on the question being asked. A map intended for navigation need not serve the same purpose as one intended to display geological formations.

This does not mean that representations are arbitrary. A map that systematically misidentifies roads would be a poor navigational map regardless of its intended purpose. Rather, usefulness requires both correspondence with relevant features and a purpose against which that correspondence can be judged.

# RC 2 — INCENTIVES AND MEASUREMENT

### Passage

Organisations frequently convert broad goals into measurable targets because measurement makes performance easier to monitor. Yet the very clarity that makes a target useful can also create a problem. Once rewards become tied to the target, employees may rationally devote effort to improving the measured quantity even when doing so does little to advance the broader objective.

This does not necessarily indicate poor motivation. The employee may simply be responding to the incentive structure created by the organisation. If the organisation rewards the metric rather than the objective that the metric was supposed to represent, optimising the metric may be the most rational available strategy.

The difficulty therefore lies not in measurement itself but in mistaking an indicator for a complete definition of success. Metrics remain valuable when they continue to track what the organisation actually cares about. When that connection weakens, measurement can produce impressive numbers while leaving the underlying objective largely unchanged.

## Q1 — **B**

Let the distance be \(d\).

Total time:

\[
\frac d5+\frac d3=6.4
\]

\[
d\left(\frac{8}{15}\right)=6.4.
\]

\[
d=6.4\cdot\frac{15}{8}=12.
\]

\[
\boxed{12\text{ km}}
\]

**Answer: B**

**Source:** CAT-level inspired  
**Topic:** Time-Speed-Distance  
**Difficulty:** 4/5  
**Recommended time:** 1.5 min  
**Shortcut:** For equal distances, use harmonic mean:
\[
\bar v=\frac{2uv}{u+v}.
\]
Then:
\[
\bar v=\frac{2(5)(3)}8=\frac{15}{4}.
\]
Total time = 6.4, so total distance:
\[
\frac{15}{4}\times6.4=24.
\]
One-way distance = 12.  
**Common trap:** Using \(4\) km/h as the average speed.  
**Key concept:** Equal-distance average speed is not arithmetic mean.  
**Alternate:** Direct time equation as above.
