# RC 1 — WHEN FAILURE TEACHES

### Passage

Modern organisations often speak of failure as though it were inherently educational. The assumption is attractive: if an unsuccessful experiment produces useful information, then failure can be transformed into a form of progress. But this interpretation overlooks an important distinction. Failure becomes informative only when the conditions of the failure can be understood.

An experiment that fails because a carefully controlled assumption was false can indeed teach us something valuable. By contrast, an experiment whose outcome is affected by numerous uncontrolled variables may tell us very little. Calling both outcomes “productive failures” simply because both were unsuccessful confuses the result with the information contained in the result.

This distinction also matters for organisational culture. Encouraging employees to take risks may generate experimentation, but rewarding failure indiscriminately can create the illusion that unsuccessful action is automatically valuable. A culture that genuinely learns from failure must instead encourage people to identify assumptions, record evidence and examine why an expected outcome did not occur.

Failure, therefore, is not itself a resource. It becomes a resource when an organisation has the intellectual and procedural capacity to extract information from it. The goal should not be to increase failure, but to make unsuccessful experiments, when they occur, more informative.

# RC 2 — PREDICTION AND JUDGMENT

### Passage

There is a recurring temptation in discussions of artificial intelligence to frame the relationship between machines and humans as a contest. If a system can perform a task faster or more accurately than people, it is assumed that human involvement should eventually disappear. Such reasoning treats performance on an isolated task as though it were equivalent to the value of the entire decision-making process.

Many important decisions, however, contain elements that cannot be reduced to the accuracy of a single prediction. A medical system may identify a statistically likely diagnosis, while the physician must still consider the patient's circumstances, preferences and tolerance for risk. A financial model may estimate the probability of default, while a lender must decide how the consequences of different decisions should be distributed.

This does not imply that human judgment is inherently superior. Human decisions can be inconsistent, biased and poorly informed. Nor does it imply that algorithmic systems should be restricted to minor tasks. Rather, the important question is what part of a decision is prediction and what part is judgment about objectives, trade-offs and consequences.

The most productive relationship between humans and intelligent systems may therefore be complementary rather than competitive. Machines can improve prediction and consistency; humans can remain responsible for determining how predictions should influence decisions when values and consequences are involved.
