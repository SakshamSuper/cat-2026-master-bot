# RC 1 — THE PROBLEM WITH PERFECT CATEGORIES

### Passage

Categories are indispensable to human reasoning because they allow us to compress an enormous amount of information into manageable forms. A biologist cannot discuss every individual organism separately; a policymaker cannot design a programme for every citizen as though no two people shared relevant characteristics. Categories make comparison and communication possible.

Yet the usefulness of a category does not imply that the boundary it creates is naturally fixed. Many categories are designed for particular purposes. A classification that is useful for taxation may be unhelpful for studying cultural identity; a classification useful in medicine may be unsuitable for describing social behaviour. The same object or person may therefore legitimately belong to different categories depending on the question being asked.

The danger arises when a category created for one purpose is treated as though it reveals an intrinsic and permanent structure of reality. Categories are tools of thought, not necessarily discoveries of nature. Their value should consequently be judged partly by how well they serve the purpose for which they were created.

# RC 2 — TECHNOLOGY AND ATTENTION

### Passage

Technological systems are often described as neutral instruments whose effects depend entirely on how people choose to use them. This description is attractive because it preserves a clear distinction between human intention and technological design. Yet technologies also shape behaviour by making certain actions easier, more visible or more rewarding than others.

This does not mean that technology determines behaviour. Users retain agency, and the same device can be incorporated into very different routines. But design can alter the environment in which choices are made. A system that constantly presents notifications, for example, changes the cost of ignoring information compared with a system that remains silent until deliberately consulted.

The important question, therefore, is not whether technology controls human behaviour, but how technological design interacts with human preferences and institutional incentives. Understanding that interaction is more useful than either extreme view: that technology determines everything, or that technology determines nothing.
