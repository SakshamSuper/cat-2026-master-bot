# RC 1 — THE IDEA OF PROGRESS

### Passage

The language of progress often carries an implicit assumption: that societies move along a single path from less developed to more developed states. Such a conception makes comparison convenient because it permits institutions, technologies and social arrangements to be ranked on one scale. But convenience can conceal the assumptions embedded in the scale itself.

A society may adopt a technology that increases productivity while simultaneously producing forms of dependence that were previously absent. Another may reject a technology not because it lacks sophistication, but because its costs conflict with values that the society considers important. To describe one society as “behind” another therefore requires more than evidence of different outcomes; it requires a prior decision about which outcomes count as improvements.

This does not mean that all forms of change are equally desirable or that comparison between societies is impossible. It means that comparisons should distinguish between **measuring change** and **evaluating change**. The first can often be empirical. The second inevitably involves criteria that require justification.

The most useful concept of progress, therefore, is not one that abandons comparison but one that makes its standards visible. A society can then ask not merely whether it has changed, but whether the direction of that change advances the ends it has reason to value.

# RC 2 — THE COST OF CERTAINTY

### Passage

Institutions often prefer certainty because decisions made under uncertainty are difficult to defend. A precise forecast, even when fragile, can appear more useful than a range of plausible outcomes. This preference is understandable: budgets, policies and schedules frequently require concrete decisions. Yet the desire for certainty can encourage organisations to suppress information about uncertainty rather than incorporate it into decision-making.

A forecast is not merely a statement about what will happen. It is also a representation of what is known, what is assumed and what remains unknown. Two forecasts may therefore have identical central estimates while differing greatly in their reliability. A forecast that openly identifies the conditions under which it might fail can be more useful than one that presents a single number with unwarranted confidence.

The problem is not that institutions should refuse to make decisions until uncertainty disappears. Such a standard would make action impossible. Rather, uncertainty should become part of the decision itself. Plans can include contingencies, thresholds for revision and indicators that signal when assumptions are no longer credible.

Good decision-making under uncertainty is therefore not the elimination of uncertainty from communication. It is the ability to act while remaining responsive to the possibility that one's assumptions may prove wrong.
