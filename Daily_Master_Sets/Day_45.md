Efficiency is often treated as an unquestionable virtue. An efficient process uses fewer resources to produce a given result, and organisations naturally seek such processes. Yet efficiency is meaningful only relative to a defined objective. A system can become highly efficient at producing something that is no longer particularly valuable.

### Passage

Historical explanations often face a difficulty that laboratory sciences can avoid: the events being explained cannot generally be reproduced under controlled conditions. Historians must therefore work with incomplete evidence, conflicting accounts and sources produced for purposes other than historical inquiry.

## Q1 — **C, 80 L**

Let original quantity be \(x\).

Initial acid:

\[
\frac38x
\]

Initial water:

\[
\frac58x
\]

In 16 L of solution removed:

Acid removed:

\[
16\cdot\frac38=6
\]

Water removed:

\[
16\cdot\frac58=10.
\]

After replacement:

\[
\text{acid}=\frac38x-6
\]

\[
\text{water}=\frac58x-10+16=\frac58x+6.
\]

Given final ratio \(3:7\):

\[
\frac{\frac38x-6}{\frac58x+6}=\frac37.
\]

Therefore:

\[
7\left(\frac38x-6\right)
=
3\left(\frac58x+6\right)
\]

\[
\frac{21x}{8}-42
=
\frac{15x}{8}+18
\]

\[
\frac{6x}{8}=60
\]

\[
x=80.
\]

\[
\boxed C
\]

**Source:** CAT-level inspired  
**Topic:** Mixtures  
**Difficulty:** 4/5  
**Recommended time:** 2 min  

**Shortcut:** Track only the component that is **not added back** — acid.

**Common trap:** Subtracting 16 L from both components instead of their respective proportions.

**Key concept:** In replacement questions, removed quantities follow the original mixture ratio.

**Alternate:** Let initial acid be \(3k\), water \(5k\), then solve after replacement.
