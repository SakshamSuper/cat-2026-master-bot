# RC 1 — THE LIMITS OF OBJECTIVITY

### Passage

Objectivity is often treated as though it were a property that information possesses independently of the processes through which that information is produced. Yet many forms of apparently objective knowledge depend upon choices about what to observe, how to classify it and which differences to regard as significant. These choices need not make the resulting knowledge arbitrary. They simply mean that objectivity can coexist with a framework that determines what becomes visible.

This distinction matters because disagreements about knowledge are sometimes described as conflicts between objective evidence and subjective interpretation. Such a description can be misleading. Even highly systematic evidence has a history of construction: instruments have been selected, categories defined and measurements standardised. The resulting evidence can be reliable while still reflecting the purposes and assumptions built into its production.

The point is not that evidence is merely a disguised opinion. Rather, recognising the conditions under which evidence is produced allows us to understand both its strength and its limits. Objectivity is not weakened by acknowledging these conditions; it is better understood through them.

# RC 2 — TECHNOLOGY AND HUMAN JUDGMENT

### Passage

Technological systems are often praised for reducing the role of human judgment. By converting decisions into rules, scores or automated procedures, they appear to replace inconsistency with uniformity. Yet reducing the visible role of judgment does not necessarily eliminate judgment itself. Decisions about which variables matter, how they should be weighted and what outcomes count as desirable must still be made somewhere.

This is particularly important when a system is used in situations where the underlying objective is contested. An automated procedure may apply its rules consistently while producing outcomes that different groups regard as fair or unfair. The consistency of the procedure therefore cannot by itself settle the legitimacy of the decision.

The appropriate response is not to reject automation. Rather, automated systems should be evaluated at two levels: whether they apply their rules reliably and whether the rules themselves embody defensible assumptions. A system can succeed at the first while failing at the second.
