### Passage

Measurement is attractive because it transforms complicated judgments into apparently precise numbers. A number allows organisations to compare outcomes, establish targets and monitor changes over time. Yet precision should not be confused with completeness: a metric captures only those aspects of a phenomenon that its designers have chosen to measure.

The problem becomes more significant when rewards are attached to a metric. People respond to incentives, often rationally, by directing greater effort toward what is rewarded. An organisation can consequently become more successful according to its chosen metric while becoming less successful according to its broader purpose.

This does not mean measurement should be abandoned. Without indicators, many decisions would be difficult to evaluate. The more sensible approach is to treat indicators as imperfect representations of objectives, use multiple measures where appropriate and periodically examine whether the measurement system creates unintended incentives.

### Passage

Technological innovation is often evaluated as though technical superiority alone determines success. But technologies do not operate in isolation. Their adoption depends on infrastructure, organisational capabilities, user habits, complementary technologies and economic incentives.

This explains why technically impressive inventions sometimes fail to spread. A new technology may require costly changes to existing systems, extensive retraining or substantial complementary investment.
