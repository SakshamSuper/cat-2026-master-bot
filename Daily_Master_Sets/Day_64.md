# RC 1 — MEASURING WHAT MATTERS

### Passage

Measurement appears to offer a way of replacing disagreement with precision. Once a phenomenon has been converted into a number, competing claims can seem easier to compare. Yet measurement does not eliminate judgment; it often moves judgment into the choice of what is measured, how it is measured, and which measurements are treated as significant.

Consider a university that evaluates its departments primarily through the number of research papers produced. The metric may be useful, but it can also alter behaviour. Researchers may have incentives to divide projects into smaller publications, favour topics that generate quick results, or avoid work whose value will take years to become visible. The metric has not merely recorded behaviour; it has become part of the environment in which behaviour occurs.

This does not make measurement useless. Without measurement, institutions may be unable to identify trends or compare outcomes. The problem arises when a measure becomes a substitute for the broader objective it was intended to represent. A university exists for more than producing papers, just as a hospital exists for more than reducing the average duration of a patient's stay.

Good measurement therefore requires a distinction between an **indicator** and the **goal represented by that indicator**. The indicator can inform judgment, but it cannot replace the judgment required to determine whether the underlying objective is actually being achieved.

# RC 2 — SPECIALISATION AND GENERAL KNOWLEDGE

### Passage

Modern institutions increasingly depend on specialised knowledge. A scientist may understand a narrow technical problem in extraordinary depth while knowing little about neighbouring fields. Such specialisation is not merely unavoidable; it has generated much of the progress associated with modern science and technology. The difficulty arises when specialisation is mistaken for a complete account of a problem.

Many real-world decisions cross disciplinary boundaries. A technological system may be technically efficient yet economically impractical; an economically attractive policy may generate legal complications; a scientifically promising intervention may fail because communities do not accept it. Expertise remains essential in each case, but no single expertise exhausts the problem.

This creates a paradox. As knowledge becomes more specialised, the need for coordination among specialists increases. Institutions therefore need mechanisms through which different forms of expertise can be brought together without pretending that they are interchangeable. The economist need not become an engineer, nor the engineer a lawyer. What matters is that each understands enough about the assumptions and limitations of the others' perspectives to participate meaningfully in a shared decision.

The solution, then, is not to reverse specialisation but to complement it with forms of general understanding. Such understanding does not mean knowing everything. It means knowing where one's own knowledge ends and where another kind of expertise becomes necessary.

# 🎯 DAY 64 — 99+ PERCENTILE SHORTCUT BANK

### 1. Profit + Discount

If:

\[
\text{Discount}=d,\quad \text{Profit}=p,
\]

don't assume CP = MP.

Use:

\[
SP=(1-d)MP=(1+p)CP.
\]

---

### 2. Time & Work

Always convert:

\[
\boxed{\text{time}\rightarrow\text{rate}}
\]

before combining workers.

---

### 3. Quadratic roots

If only expressions such as:

\[
\alpha^2+\beta^2,\quad
\alpha^3+\beta^3,\quad
(\alpha-\beta)^2
\]

are asked, **do not solve the quadratic**.

Use:

\[
\alpha+\beta=-\frac ba,\qquad
\alpha\beta=\frac ca.
\]

---

### 4. Range

For expressions like:

\[
\frac{x^2+6x+11}{x^2+6x+10},
\]

try:

\[
\boxed{\text{numerator}=\text{denominator}+\text{constant}}.
\]

Then complete the square.

---

### 5. Cyclic quadrilateral

Memorise:

\[
\boxed{AC\cdot BD=AB\cdot CD+BC\cdot DA}.
\]

Ptolemy can turn a geometry question into a 10-second arithmetic question.

---

### 6. Perfect-square divisors

For:

\[
N=p_1^{a_1}p_2^{a_2}\cdots,
\]

number of square divisors:

\[
\boxed{\left(\left\lfloor\frac{a_1}{2}\right\rfloor+1\right)
\left(\left\lfloor\frac{a_2}{2}\right\rfloor+1\right)\cdots}
\]

---

### 7. The 99+ trick from Q12

Whenever you see:

\[
xy+x+y=k,
\]

immediately write:

\[
\boxed{(x+1)(y+1)=k+1}.
\]

This is one of those CAT patterns where **recognition beats calculation**.
