# RC 1 — THE PROBLEM WITH SIMPLICITY

### Passage

Simplicity is often celebrated as a virtue in public reasoning. A simple explanation is easier to communicate, easier to remember and less demanding to apply. Yet simplicity has a cost: reducing a complicated phenomenon to a small number of variables necessarily leaves something out. The question is not whether simplification occurs, but whether what has been omitted matters for the purpose at hand.

This distinction becomes important when simple explanations acquire authority beyond their original context. A model developed to clarify one relationship may later be treated as a complete account of a much larger phenomenon. Its elegance can then become a liability, because the very features that made it useful—its exclusions and assumptions—are no longer visible.

The alternative is not to reject simple models in favour of infinitely complicated ones. Complexity can obscure relationships just as easily as excessive simplicity can. What is required is an awareness of the boundary of a model: where it works, where it stops working, and what kinds of questions it was never designed to answer.

A useful model, therefore, is not one that captures everything. It is one whose simplifications are appropriate to its intended purpose and whose limitations remain visible to those who use it.

# RC 2 — THE ECONOMICS OF ATTENTION

### Passage

Attention is commonly described as a scarce resource because individuals cannot process every available stimulus with equal intensity. Digital environments appear to intensify this scarcity by presenting users with an almost continuous stream of information. It is tempting, therefore, to conclude that the central problem of modern media is simply an excess of information.

But information abundance is not necessarily the same as attentional impoverishment. People have always developed mechanisms for selecting what deserves attention. The more significant change may be that digital systems increasingly participate in that selection. Recommendation algorithms, notifications and ranking systems do not merely provide information; they influence which information is encountered first and which is encountered repeatedly.

This does not mean that users have become passive. People can ignore recommendations, change settings or deliberately seek unfamiliar material. Yet the architecture through which choices are presented affects the costs of those decisions. An option that requires deliberate search is not functionally equivalent to one placed immediately before the user.

The question is therefore less about whether technology creates too much information and more about how technological systems distribute the opportunities to notice, ignore and explore. Attention is shaped not only by individual preferences but also by the environments through which those preferences operate.

\]

Combine:

\[
\frac{x+y}{xy}=\frac1{12}.
\]

Thus:

\[
xy=12x+12y.
\]

Add 144:

\[
xy-12x-12y+144=144.
\]

Factor:

\[
(x-12)(y-12)=144.
\]

Since \(x,y\) are positive and the right side is positive, both factors can be positive or both negative.

But if both are negative:

\[
x<12,\ y<12,
\]

then:

\[
\frac1x+\frac1y>\frac1{12}+\frac1{12}>\frac1{12},
\]

impossible.

Therefore both factors are positive.

So every positive divisor \(d\) of 144 gives:

\[
x=12+d,\qquad y=12+\frac{144}{d}.
\]

Number of ordered pairs equals number of positive divisors of 144.

\[
144=2^4\cdot3^2.
\]

Hence:

\[
(4+1)(2+1)=15.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{15}.
\]

**Answer: B**
