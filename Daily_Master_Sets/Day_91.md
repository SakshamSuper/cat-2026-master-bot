# RC 1 — EXPERTISE AND SELECTIVITY

### Passage

Expertise is sometimes described simply as knowing more than others. Yet the more important distinction may lie elsewhere. Experts are often better at identifying which information deserves attention in a particular situation. Their advantage is therefore not merely the size of their knowledge base but the organisation of that knowledge.

This explains why an expert can occasionally appear faster than a novice despite considering a problem more deeply. Experience has created distinctions that the expert can recognise almost automatically. A novice, by contrast, may spend considerable time treating relevant and irrelevant information as equally important.

Such selectivity is not infallible. Familiar patterns can become a source of error when a new situation resembles an old one only superficially. Expertise therefore requires not only rapid pattern recognition but also the ability to recognise when a familiar pattern should be questioned.

# RC 2 — TECHNOLOGY AND INSTITUTIONS

### Passage

Technological innovation is often presented as an autonomous force that reshapes institutions. Such descriptions overlook the fact that technologies enter environments already structured by rules, incentives and habits. A technology may make a particular action easier, but whether people actually adopt that action depends partly on how existing institutions respond.

This helps explain why identical technologies can produce divergent consequences. A communication system might decentralise authority in one organisation while strengthening central control in another. The difference need not lie in the technology itself; it may lie in the institutional incentives surrounding its use.

Technological capability, therefore, should not be equated with technological impact. To understand the latter, one must examine both what the technology permits and what the surrounding institutional structure rewards, discourages or normalises.
